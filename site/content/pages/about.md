I'm a Master's student in sport analytics, and I analyse cricket and football with data. This site is where that work lives: match breakdowns, player studies, tactical pieces and longer research projects, each one built on data I can point to and methods I can defend.

My approach is simple to describe and harder to do well. Start with a question worth answering. Find data that can actually answer it. Clean it, validate it, and be honest about what it can and can't tell you. Then write it up so that someone who loves the sport — not just someone who loves spreadsheets — wants to read to the end.

## What I actually do

The work on this site reflects what I spend my time on:

- **Cricket analytics in R.** I work with ball-by-ball data from the [Cricsheet](https://cricsheet.org/) archive through the `cricketdata` package — reconstructing innings, validating against the scorecard, and breaking performances down by phase, matchup and scoring pattern.
- **Football analysis in Python.** I use StatsBomb's open event data with `statsbombpy` and `mplsoccer` to answer tactical and player questions — for example, [comparing how Rodri and Declan Rice progressed the ball at Euro 2024](/projects/rodri-rice-euro-2024/).
- **Building tools and visualisations** that turn raw event and tracking data into something a coach, recruiter or fan can read at a glance.

## How I think about analysis

A number is only as good as the method behind it, so I show the method. Every data-led piece on this site carries a short sources-and-methodology note. Where a figure is verified, I say so; where something is interpretation, I say that too; and where the data simply isn't available, I'd rather tell you than guess. If a chart would need data I don't have, it doesn't go in.

That discipline matters most when the story is exciting. It's easy to let a spectacular scoreline write the analysis for you. The harder, better job is to work out *why* it happened and what the evidence can actually support.
